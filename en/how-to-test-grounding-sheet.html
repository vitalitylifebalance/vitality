How to know if your grounding sheet is working

There are three levels of testing, from least to most reliable: (1) checking the fabric's electrical continuity with a multimeter, (2) verifying that your home's outlet has a real ground connection, and (3) measuring your own body voltage before and after connecting. Only the third confirms that the whole system — fabric, cable, outlet, and your body — is actually working.

In short, here's the full protocol:

  1. Test 1 — Fabric continuity: with the multimeter in ohms mode, confirm that the sheet's surface conducts electricity through to the cable terminal.
  2. Test 2 — Outlet ground connection: with the multimeter in AC volts mode, confirm that your outlet's ground actually works (expected live-to-ground reading: ~230 V in the UK/EU, ~120 V in the US/Canada).
  3. Test 3 — Body voltage: with the multimeter's black probe on the ground point of the outlet you already verified, measure the voltage induced in your body without touching the sheet (normally 1–5 V AC) and while touching it connected (it should drop sharply, typically below 0.25 V AC).

If all three tests pass, your grounding sheet works: you'll see the voltage drop on the screen, in real time. Let's go test by test, but first, the important part.

A word of caution before you start: safety with outlets and multimeters

Read this before you put a multimeter near an outlet

Never touch the multimeter's metal probe tips while measuring, and never measure with wet hands.

Never open or physically tamper with the outlet or its wiring box. Every test in this guide is done by inserting only the multimeter's probes into the visible holes of the socket.

Always use AC volts mode to measure the outlet. A multimeter accidentally set to amps (A) mode on a live outlet creates a short circuit: it can blow the device's fuse, trip the breaker, or cause an electrical arc.

If your wiring is old, has visible splices, or you simply don't feel confident, consult a certified/licensed electrician. Measuring an outlet is simple, but no measurement is worth more than your safety.

If you have a pacemaker or other implanted medical device, consult your doctor before using grounding products.

With that clear, let's continue.

Is a regular multimeter enough, or do you need the manufacturer's tester?

A basic digital multimeter in the 8–15€ range — an Aneng, a Uni-T, or similar, with AC/DC volts and ohms functions — is enough for all three tests in this article. The proprietary tester that some brands sell (or bundle for free) only adds convenience: an LED that lights up instead of a number to read. It doesn't add precision.

This is where we have to be radically honest, because it's where the sector's marketing generates the most noise. Our own grounding sheet includes a tester so you can check the connection without tools, and it's handy to have. But a manufacturer's tester doesn't do anything a generic multimeter from any hardware store can't do — in fact it does less, since it gives you no numeric values and won't let you check the outlet or your own body voltage. If you already have a multimeter in your toolbox, you don't need to buy anything else at all to verify your sheet, whatever brand it is. And if you don't have one, for about 10€ you'll get a tool that will also come in handy for batteries, fuses, and power strips for the rest of your life.

Test 1 — How to check the fabric's continuity, step by step

This first test answers a simple question: does the sheet's surface conduct electricity through to the cable? It's the test behind manufacturers' “earthing conductivity testers,” done properly, with numbers.

  1. Set the multimeter to ohms mode (Ω), on the 200 kΩ range or higher (or auto-range, if your model has it). Don't use the audible continuity mode yet — you'll see why in a moment.
  2. Connect the cable to the sheet via its clip, as if you were about to use it, but without plugging it into the wall.
  3. Rest one probe on the metal terminal at the free end of the cable (the part that would go into the outlet).
  4. Rest the other probe on the fabric's surface and press firmly, moving it slightly so it contacts several conductive fibres. This is key: the silver fibres are woven into a grid inside the cotton, and a light surface touch might miss all of them.
  5. Read the value. If you get a stable figure around 100 kΩ (the cable's safety resistor) plus a few hundred Ω from the fabric itself, there's continuity: the fabric conducts.
  6. Repeat in 3–4 different spots on the sheet (centre, corners, foot area) to confirm the whole surface is connected to the grid.

A note on materials: the reason a silver-thread sheet gives such a clean reading comes down to the physics of the metal itself. Silver has an electrical conductivity of 63×10⁶ S/m versus 1.4×10⁶ S/m for stainless steel — roughly 45 times higher, according to reference conductivity values from the CRC Handbook of Chemistry and Physics — and that difference also explains why resistance readings vary so much between brands and materials. If you want the full breakdown, we cover it with data in our comparison of silver versus stainless steel in grounding sheets.

Why doesn't continuity beep even though the sheet is brand new?

Because the connection cable has a built-in ~100,000 Ω (100 kΩ) safety resistor that protects you from shocks if the outlet were ever faulty. With that resistance in series, a multimeter in “audible continuity” mode won't beep — that mode only sounds below a few dozen ohms. You have to read the value in ohms mode, not wait for the beep.

This is, by far, the most common false negative, and the one that sends the most perfectly functional sheets back in a return box. The user's logic is flawless: “continuity mode, probe on the fabric, probe on the cable... no beep, so it doesn't conduct, so it's broken.” But there's a hidden piece in that reasoning: that 100 kΩ resistor is a deliberate safety feature, present in the cables of practically every serious manufacturer in the sector, that limits the current that could reach your body in the event of an electrical fault. The multimeter reads it as “a lot of resistance” and stays silent, even though continuity exists and is perfectly functional for draining the static microvoltages we're talking about here.

The practical rule: on a grounding sheet, the beep means nothing; the number is everything. If, in ohms mode, you read a stable value around 100 kΩ (plus the fabric's small resistance), your sheet and cable are fine.

Test 2 — How to check whether your home's outlet has a real ground connection

Your sheet can only drain charge if the outlet you plug it into has a working ground connection. And this isn't a given: old, non-grounded electrical installations are a well-known issue in buildings built before modern electrical codes made grounding mandatory — the specific rules and dates vary by country, but the underlying problem doesn't. In many older properties the ground was never wired in at all, or a ground terminal exists but is “dead” — with no real connection to the building's earth stake. A perfect sheet plugged into a dead ground does absolutely nothing.

The test, respecting the safety notice above. The logic is the same everywhere — measure between a live conductor and the ground point — but the plug type and the reading you should expect change depending on where you are:

  1. Set the multimeter to AC volts (V~), 600 V range or higher if it isn't auto-range.
  2. Insert one probe into one of the outlet's current holes or slots — live or neutral; the exact pin layout depends on your plug type (see below).
  3. Touch the other probe to the outlet's ground point — again, its exact location depends on your plug type (see below).
  4. Read the value and repeat with the other current hole or slot. Between line/hot and ground you should read roughly your region's mains voltage; between neutral and ground, a value close to 0 V.

Where to find the ground point, and what to expect, by region:

  • UK (Type G, 230 V): on a three-pin plug, the earth pin is the longer top-centre pin. Between the live pin and the earth pin you should read ~230 V.
  • Continental Europe (Schuko / Type F, 230 V): the ground point is one of the two metal side clips visible on the rim of the socket. Between one of the two round current holes and the side ground clips you should read ~220–230 V.
  • USA / Canada (Type B, NEMA, 120 V): the ground point is the round hole below the two flat slots. Between the shorter (hot) flat slot and the ground hole you should read ~120 V — a genuinely different figure from the European ~230 V, not a typo, tied to the 120 V North American mains standard.

Interpretation, wherever you are: if one of the two combinations shows roughly your region's expected mains voltage (~230 V in the UK/EU, ~120 V in the US/Canada), that outlet's ground is wired to a real earth reference. If both combinations read close to 0 V (or erratic, very low values), that outlet has no functional ground, and no sheet on the market can ground you through it. The fix is to try a different outlet in the property (ground sometimes only reaches the kitchen and bathrooms), or to have an electrician check the installation.

An honest note on the limits of this test: a correct mains-voltage reading rules out a “dead” ground, but it doesn't distinguish a properly earthed ground from a ground bonded to neutral — a shortcut sometimes found in informal rewiring of older properties, which also produces a normal-looking reading. If your wiring is old or you have any doubt, the definitive check is done by a certified/licensed electrician with the right instruments.

Test 3 — How to measure your body voltage before and after connecting (the star test)

Set the multimeter to AC volts, connect the black probe (COM) to a real ground reference — the ground point of the outlet you already verified in Test 2 — and hold the red probe between your fingers. Without touching the sheet, it's normal to read between 1 and 5 V: your body acts as an antenna for the ambient electric field at mains frequency (50 Hz in the UK/EU, 60 Hz in the US/Canada). Lying down on the connected sheet, the reading should drop sharply, typically below 0.25 V. That drop is proof you're actually grounding.

This is the only test that verifies the whole system actually working: fabric + cable + outlet + contact with your skin. It's the method documented in the sector's reference guides, and the key is the reference point: you're measuring your body against ground, not against thin air. Step by step:

  1. Multimeter in AC volts, low range (20 V or auto-range).
  2. Connect the black probe (COM) to the ground point of the outlet you already verified in Test 2: rest it only on the ground point (the Schuko side clip, the UK earth pin, or the NEMA ground hole, depending on your plug type), without inserting anything into the live or neutral holes (consistent with the safety notice above).
  3. Hold the red probe between your fingers, making good contact with your skin.
  4. Note the reading without touching the sheet. In a typical bedroom, with wiring in the walls and devices plugged in, you'll usually see between 1 and 5 V AC (it can be higher in rooms with lots of devices). Don't worry: this is induced voltage with no meaningful current — your body acting as an antenna.
  5. Plug in the sheet, lie down or rest your bare forearm on the fabric, and watch the screen. If the sheet's plug occupies the same outlet you're measuring from, use the second slot of a double socket or another verified outlet in the room, and keep the probe resting on the ground point while you lie down.
  6. The reading should drop sharply, typically to under 0.25 V AC, the moment your skin contacts the conductive fibres. Lift your arm, and it rises; rest it again, and it falls. That switch you see on the screen is your ground connection, live, in real time.

An important note: the reference values (1–5 V unconnected, under 0.25 V connected) are only valid when measuring against a real ground reference, as described here. If you leave the reference probe “floating in the air,” readings depend on cable position and the multimeter's impedance: they're erratic, not reproducible, and can produce false negatives.

If the drop happens, the uncertainty is over: your grounding sheet works, measured with high-school physics. If it doesn't happen, go to the table below.

How many ohms should a grounding sheet read?

There's no single “correct” number, because the reading depends on the cable — its ~100 kΩ safety resistor — plus the fabric's own resistance, usually an extra few hundred ohms. What matters isn't the absolute value, but that measurable, stable continuity exists, and that the body-voltage test shows a clear drop (from 1–5 V to under 0.25 V).

Put another way: if you're searching for “how many ohms should a grounding sheet read” expecting a magic number, you're asking the wrong question. A reading of 100.3 kΩ and one of 102 kΩ are equally good. An infinite reading (“OL”) is bad. And a great ohms reading with an ungrounded outlet is still a system that doesn't work. That's why the order of the tests matters, and why the final verdict always comes from body voltage, not resistance.
